Pay-by-Bank Widget — Worked Example

Embeddable checkout widget · 60-minute full-stack round · desktop-first, mobile flagged

The server owns the money state, the browser only ever holds values that are safe to leak, and every hop that could move money twice is protected by something atomic.

How to use this. Part One is what you should be able to produce unprompted in the first thirty minutes. Part Two is what you reach for when the interviewer probes. If you only have one evening, learn Part One cold and skim Part Two's headings so you know what you own.

PART ONE — the answer you produce · requirements, entities, credentials, interfaces, diagrams

1. Requirements and scope

Functional

  • Merchant embeds a widget into an existing checkout. We control the widget's content and flow, not the merchant.
  • User selects a bank inside the widget, authenticates at the bank, confirms, returns.
  • Neither we nor the merchant ever see banking credentials.
  • Final outcome delivered asynchronously to the merchant's backend.

Out of scope, said out loud

  • Repeat payments. Every payment is a fresh authorization. Deletes the mandate store.
  • Refunds UI, merchant dashboard, SEO, initial-load perf.

Non-functional

  • Isolation. Merchant page cannot read into the widget, widget cannot read the merchant page.
  • Exactly-once money movement. Refresh, double-click, back button, duplicated redirects, retried webhooks.
  • Merchant page survival. Losing a live checkout mid-flow is a product failure.
  • Honest async UI. The rail answers on its own schedule.
  • Accessibility and i18n, covered in section 16.

Rail and risk, decided up front

ACH, and we underwrite the return risk. Once we say succeeded that is terminal for the merchant.

The core tension, name it now. The user wants one click and instant confirmation. The rail is slow, pull-based, and reversible for up to 60 days. Every interesting decision in this design is about hiding that gap without lying.

3. Credentials

Two kinds, and keeping them apart is the point. Secrets are long-lived, server-only, and identify a party. Tokens are short-lived, scoped, and identify a thing in flight.

Secrets — no TTL, never in a browser, rotated not expired

SecretHeld byAnswers the question
merchant_api_secretMerchant backend "Is this really merchant X." This is what makes the amount server-asserted rather than something a browser can claim.
our client_secret at the bankOur backend "Is this really the platform you registered." This, not the nonce, is what buys the access token.
webhook_signing_secretBoth backends "Did this webhook really come from us." Two active at once during rotation.

Tokens — TTL, scoped, some safe in a browser

TokenLivesTTLAuthorizes
session_tokenbrowser30 min Read and advance one session. Cannot change the amount, cannot create sessions.
oauth_state (nonce)URLs + our DB10 min, single use Nothing at the bank. Purely our own gate. Proves the callback belongs to a flow we started and says which session.

The test for anything browser-held. Not "is it hidden" but "what is the worst thing someone can do with it." A stolen session_token lets an attacker pay the merchant from their own bank account. Nobody runs that attack. The 30-minute TTL is a product decision about how long a user hunts for a password. The security comes from scope. It is enforced by expires_at in the WHERE clause of every query, never by an HTTP header.

4. Interfaces

Split by who holds what credential, not by resource. The merchant touches only the first two surfaces below. The /v1/widget/* endpoints are called by our own SPA and the merchant never sees them, but they are where the nonce burn and the money movement happen.

Merchant backend — REST, merchant_api_secret

No :id in these paths. The token resolves the session server-side, which deletes an entire bug class because there is no identifier in a URL to tamper with. And there is no /success endpoint — the client reports that it came back from a redirect, the server decides what that means.

Merchant frontend — our JS SDK

  sessionToken,                       // from their backend
  onSuccess: (result) => {},          // required
  onExit:    (err, meta) => {},       // required
  onEvent:   (name, meta) => {},      // optional, funnel telemetry

Deliberately small. Every option that could change what gets charged is absent, because the amount lives on the session.

Webhooks — us to the merchant

payment_session.succeeded          seconds after Pay · terminal · may ship
payment_session.failed             risk declined, or the bank said no
payment_session.canceled

No payment_session.returned, because we underwrite. No payment_session.completed, because it would arrive three seconds before something actionable and every event is a permanent API commitment.

The merchant's total obligation

Two callbacks they must implement, onSuccess and onExit. Two endpoints, one they call and one they host. The hosted webhook handler is most of the work and it is the only thing that mutates their order. They could not observe a bank credential if they tried.

6. Architecture

Two paths, one picture. The blue path is the browser dance whose only job is to get consent without the bank ever being framed and without the merchant page unloading. The green and amber path is the money, which is entirely server-to-server and finishes minutes to days after the user has closed the tab. The red arrow is the one interviewers listen for. The browser only ever carries a one-time code, and our backend redeems it directly against the bank with our client_secret and PKCE verifier, so an access_token never exists anywhere a merchant or a user could see it. Draw it or the whole front-channel story is unfalsifiable.

7. 30-second recap script

The merchant's backend creates a payment session with our API secret, which is what makes the amount server-asserted rather than something a browser can claim. That returns a session token scoped to exactly one session and one amount, safe to hand to the browser because the worst it can do is pay the merchant. Their page loads our SDK, which injects an iframe from our origin, and that iframe is the isolation boundary in both directions.

When the user picks a bank we open a popup on our own origin, which 302s to the bank. The popup matters because the bank cannot be framed and because destroying a live checkout page is a conversion disaster. The bank returns to our origin with a code and a single-use nonce we generated. We burn that nonce with one atomic conditional update before exchanging the code, so a double-click or a prefetch cannot exchange twice, and then the server pushes authorized down the SSE stream the still-alive iframe has held open since boot. The popup never has to reach the iframe directly, which matters because they sit in different storage partitions.

Coming back means consent, not payment. We read the balance, score the risk, and only then insert a payment row whose unique constraint on session id is the real double-charge defense, writing the outbox event in the same transaction. The rail answers in days, not milliseconds, so we deliver a signed, at-least-once, deliberately unordered webhook to the merchant, backed by a cursor endpoint because push-only integrations are never complete. We call succeeded terminal and absorb ACH returns ourselves, which is a business decision, not a technical one. Credentials only ever pass between the user's browser and the bank, and the access token never touches a browser.

Three lines to keep in your head. The server owns the money state, the client only renders it. · The client is a courier, not a judge. · Push the invariant down to the layer that can enforce it.

Three things to notice out loud

The merchant is absent from steps 2 through 21. They create a session and they receive a webhook. That total absence from the OAuth leg is the product.

window.close() is the last thing, not the first. The popup is the only browsing context holding the nonce, so it has to stay alive long enough to hand it over. Closing on the bank's redirect would strand the flow.

Every credential upgrade happens on a server. Step 0 turns a secret into a scoped token, step 12 turns a code into an access token. Neither could happen in a browser. That is the gradient in motion.

Naming collision, and it will bite you live. The OAuth spec forces the nonce to be called state in the URL. If your session table also has a state column you will say the word twice meaning two things and confuse yourself mid-sentence. Name the session column status, as Stripe does, and reserve state for the nonce. Cheap fix, real payoff under pressure.

Never in localStorage. Never in the merchant page. The browser is the transport, and the nonce exists precisely because that transport is an untrusted courier.

Why step 6 and not step 0

The nonce is scoped to one OAuth attempt, not one session. A 30-minute session can produce several attempts. User picks Chase, bails, picks Wells Fargo. Bank times out, they retry.

Minting at step 0 breaks two things. The nonce would have to survive the full 30 minutes, stretching a deliberately 10-minute window. And a retry would reuse an already-burned nonce, so you would either fail the retry or weaken single-use, which is the property protecting you. Fresh per attempt gives you retry safety for free, and the new nonce invalidates the old one so an abandoned OAuth URL cannot be resumed by anyone who captured it.

How to answer if asked which. Take B for the simplicity and lean on the nonce. Reach for A if you need the callback to work while the exchange is slow enough to want a spinner. Knowing that the bank's redirect is always a GET, and that you therefore do not get to choose the method, is the underlying point.

9. Exactly-once, in three places

Money systems fail by doing things twice. There are exactly three places that can double here, and each needs a different mechanism. Learn them as a set, because interviewers will pick one and push.

Place 1 — the merchant creates the same session twice

Their retry logic fires, or their user double-submits. Fixed with a client-supplied Idempotency-Key.

-- on POST /v1/payment_sessions
INSERT INTO idempotency_key (key, merchant_id, request_hash)
VALUES ($1, $2, $3)
ON CONFLICT (key, merchant_id) DO NOTHING;

-- 0 rows inserted -> this key was seen before.
--   same request_hash  -> return the stored response, 200.
--   different hash     -> 422. same key, different body is a client bug.

The request_hash check matters. Without it, a merchant reusing a key with a new amount silently gets the old session and charges the wrong amount.

Place 2 — the OAuth callback fires twice

Double-click, browser prefetch, user hits back and forward. This is the classic and it is where candidates write the racy version.

The wrong version

row = SELECT * FROM oauth_state
        WHERE state = $1;
if (row.used_at != null) reject();
token = exchangeWithBank(code);   <-- slow
UPDATE oauth_state
   SET used_at = now()
 WHERE state = $1;

Two concurrent callbacks both read used_at IS NULL, both pass the check, both exchange the code. The window is as wide as a network call to a bank, which is hundreds of milliseconds. This is not a theoretical race.

The right version

UPDATE oauth_state
   SET used_at = now()
 WHERE state = $1
   AND used_at IS NULL
   AND expires_at > now();
-- assert rows_affected == 1, else reject

token = exchangeWithBank(code);   after the claim

Claim first, then do the slow thing. One atomic conditional update. The loser gets zero rows and is rejected. If the exchange then fails, the nonce stays burned and the user retries with a fresh one, which is correct.

MomentWhat happens

Why the nonce is server-generated, always. A client-generated nonce is worthless for CSRF. The check that counts is "did I generate this and have I not yet used it," and only the server can answer that. A client comparing "what I sent versus what I got back" proves nothing, because an attacker controls the client. The client is a courier, not a judge.

Place 3 — the debit is submitted twice

The most expensive one. Two app servers both process a confirm, or a retry fires after a timeout where you never learned whether the rail accepted it.

BEGIN;
  -- the partial unique index on payment makes this the whole defense
  INSERT INTO payment (payment_id, session_id, amount_minor, state)
  VALUES ($1, $2, $3, 'submitting')
  ON CONFLICT (session_id) DO NOTHING;
  -- 0 rows -> someone else owns this debit. return their result, do not submit.

  UPDATE payment_session SET state = 'payment_submitted' WHERE session_id = $2;
  INSERT INTO webhook_outbox (...) VALUES (...);   -- same transaction, see section 11
COMMIT;

-- only now, outside the transaction, call the rail with a
-- deterministic idempotency reference derived from payment_id.
The honest caveat, and volunteer it. The gap between COMMIT and the rail call is not transactional. If you crash there, you have a submitting row and no idea whether the rail got it. There is no way to close this with a database. You close it with a reconciliation job that queries the rail by your deterministic reference for anything stuck in submitting past a threshold. Every real payments system has this job.

10. Isolation and the three security boundaries

They are separate mechanisms solving separate problems. Do not blur them.

1. The iframe

Same-origin policy is what stops the merchant page from reading the widget's DOM, JS, or the session_token in its memory. It also stops the widget from reading their page. It is a two-way wall and both directions matter for a merchant's own security review.

2. postMessage

Pin the exact target origin on send. Check event.origin against an allowlist on receive, with exact string equality.

Never origin.includes('merchant.com'). merchant.com.attacker.io passes that check.

3. The CSRF nonce

Covered in section 9. Server-generated, session-bound, single-use, burned atomically.

Plus: the origin allowlist

Each merchant registers their domains. The widget refuses to boot if the embedding origin is not on the list, enforced with a frame-ancestors CSP on the widget document itself.

Without this, any site that gets hold of a live session_token could embed your widget and run a convincing branded phishing page against a real session. This is a real attack and it is easy to forget, and it is the reason a browser-scoped token is not sufficient on its own.

The postMessage frames, in full

FrameDirectionPayload and why
READYwidget → host{sessionId}. Booted, stop the spinner.
RESIZEwidget → host{sessionId, height}. The parent sets the iframe height and cannot know our content height. Fires on every step change. Easy to forget, very visible when missing.
EVENTwidget → host{sessionId, name}. Telemetry for their funnel analytics.
SUCCESSwidget → host{sessionId, status, amountMinor, currency, institutionName, accountLast4}. Display data only. No credential.
EXITwidget → host{sessionId, status, errorCode?}. Closed, expired, or failed.
OPEN / CLOSEhost → widget{}. Merchant's own button or dismiss control.

Every frame carries sessionId so a message from a stale widget instance is discarded rather than applied.

Two things to say about SUCCESS. It carries no credential, unlike Link's SUCCESS which carries a public_token, because nothing is handed to the merchant here. And onSuccess is permission to render a confirmation screen, not permission to ship. Their backend acts on the webhook, never on this callback.
What the merchant never sees. The entire OAuth leg. The popup opening, the bank redirect, the callback, the state coming home on our SSE stream. That conversation is between our iframe and our popup, both on our origin. From the merchant page's view the widget goes quiet for ninety seconds and then emits SUCCESS.

11. Webhook delivery and the merchant handler

What we send, and what we deliberately do not

Send these

payment_session.succeeded    seconds after Pay · terminal · may ship
payment_session.failed       risk declined, or bank said no
payment_session.canceled
payment_session.expired

The rule is every server-confirmed transition a merchant could act on. Ship the goods, send the receipt, release the cart.

Also no payment_session.returned, by the same logic as the state machine. We underwrite, so a return is our problem and the merchant never hears about it.

Do not send these

widget.opened
institution.selected
bank_auth.started

That is telemetry, not integration. Sending it trains merchants to ignore your events, and every event you send is an event you must keep sending forever. A webhook is a permanent API commitment.

Note what is missing given our scope. There is no payment_session.returned to the merchant, because we decided succeeded is terminal and we eat returns. Internally we still record it.

Delivery — the transactional outbox

The naive version has a real bug, and it is worth being able to name it.

The dual-write problem. If you commit the state change and then send the webhook, a crash in between means the merchant never learns and their order hangs forever. If you send first and then commit, a failed commit means you told them about a payment that did not happen. There is no ordering of two independent writes that is safe.

The fix. Write the event to webhook_outbox inside the same database transaction as the state change. Now it is one write, atomic by construction. A separate worker polls the outbox and delivers. If the worker dies, the row is still there.

Retry schedule and guarantees

Retry

Exponential backoff on any non-2xx or timeout.

5s → 30s → 2m → 10m → 1h → 3h → 6h
   → hourly out to 24h, then give up

Per-attempt timeout of 5 seconds. Without it, one slow merchant occupies your delivery workers and degrades every other merchant. This is a shared-resource failure mode worth naming.

After the last attempt, mark the endpoint unhealthy, email the merchant, and let them self-serve recovery through GET /v1/events?after={cursor}.

Guarantees, stated honestly

  • At-least-once. Every event carries an event_id. The merchant must dedupe on it. Say this in your docs, because they will not do it otherwise.
  • Not ordered. Do not promise ordering. Retries reorder by construction, and a promise you cannot keep is worse than no promise.
  • Self-describing. Because there is no ordering, every event body carries the full current session state. A merchant receiving a stale event can compare and discard.
  • GET is the truth. The webhook is a wake-up call, not the record.

Signing

Webhook-Timestamp: 1730000000
Webhook-Signature: v1=<hex hmac_sha256(secret, timestamp + "." + raw_body)>, v1=<second key during rotation>

Merchant verifies:
  1. timestamp within 5 minutes of now      -- blocks replay
  2. HMAC matches, compared in CONSTANT TIME -- naive == leaks the signature byte by byte
  3. event_id not already processed          -- dedupe

Sign over the raw body bytes, not the parsed and re-serialized JSON. Key ordering and whitespace differ between serializers and the signature will not match. This is the single most common webhook integration bug.

The alternative worth naming. Asymmetric signing, where we hold a private key and publish the public key at a JWKS URL, removes the shared-secret-leak problem and scales better across thousands of merchants. Cost is more integration work on their side. The industry mostly chose HMAC for that reason. Knowing both exist and why the simpler one won is the good answer.

What the merchant does with it

Worth walking through, because it is what motivates three fields that otherwise look arbitrary. Their schema is essentially one column.

orders (
  order_id             pk,
  status,                    -- pending_payment | paid | payment_failed
  payment_session_id         -- our id, stored when they created the session
)
POST /webhooks/paybybank            their endpoint, our delivery worker calls it

1. verify the RAW body against the HMAC, check timestamp freshness
2. dedupe
     INSERT INTO processed_events (event_id) ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING
     if 0 rows -> return 200 immediately, already handled
3. find the order
     SELECT * FROM orders WHERE payment_session_id = $1
4. apply, guarded so it cannot move backwards
     UPDATE orders SET status = 'paid'
      WHERE order_id = $1 AND status = 'pending_payment'
5. return 200 FAST
6. enqueue fulfillment and the receipt email as async jobs

Each step maps to something we designed

  • Step 3 joins on merchant_reference or session_id. That is the entire reason merchant_reference exists in our schema. It is their foreign key, carried through our system so the webhook can find its way home.
  • Step 2 is why we send event_id. We deliver at-least-once, so the same event will arrive twice eventually. Without this they ship twice.
  • Step 4's AND status = 'pending_payment' is why we do not promise ordering. A retried stale event cannot drag a paid order back to pending. The guard fails and the update is a harmless no-op.
  • Step 5 before step 6 is why we time out at five seconds. Fulfillment inline means our worker blocks, they hit our timeout, we retry, and they fulfill twice.

The line to say out loud

The browser's onSuccess callback must never write to their database. It is a UI hint so they can render a confirmation screen. The webhook is the only thing that mutates order state, and GET /v1/payment_sessions/{id} is the tiebreaker if they ever doubt it.

Ownership, stated precisely. The Order is theirs and we never see it. The PaymentSession is ours, and they can read it but only mutate it through our API. The Payment rows are ours and internal, and they never see those at all. Ownership is about who can write. Visibility is about who can read. Those are separate questions and it is worth answering them separately.

12. Rails, risk, and why succeeded is terminal

Why authorized and payment_submitted are separate

This is the single most common state-machine mistake on this question. Coming back from the bank means we have consent. It does not mean money moved. Nothing has been debited.

Between those two states we do real work. We read the balance, we score risk, and only then do we submit to the rail. Collapse them into one state and you have no place to put any of that, and no way to describe what is happening when the balance check takes four seconds.

Why succeeded is terminal here

On raw ACH it would not be. A debit can return for insufficient funds within two business days, and a consumer can claim it was unauthorized for sixty calendar days.

We made it terminal by a business decision, not a technical one. We absorb returns. That is exactly what a platform like this sells, converting a slow reversible rail into something a merchant can treat as good funds.

And this is what lets succeeded fire in seconds. If we waited for settlement, the merchant would still sit on their hands for three days and our underwriting would have bought them certainty but no speed. Because we are taking the loss anyway, the honest moment to promise is right after the risk check passes and the rail accepts. Underwriting is what converts a three-day answer into a three-second one.
Be honest about the consequence. Terminal for the merchant does not mean terminal for us. Internally the Payment row still gets a returned outcome and feeds our loss ledger. We are hiding the reversal, not eliminating it. Say this, because "succeeded is terminal" without the caveat sounds like you do not know ACH returns exist.

13. The redirect problem, and why a popup

This is the deep dive that separates a good answer from a transcription. Start from the constraint.

The constraint. A bank login page cannot render inside your iframe. Every real bank sends X-Frame-Options: DENY or CSP frame-ancestors 'self'. And even if one did not, asking a user to type bank credentials into a third-party iframe embedded in a merchant page is exactly the phishing pattern, and it destroys your "we never see credentials" claim, because the embedding page could overlay it.

So the bank must be in a top-level browsing context. There are only two of those available to you. A new window, or the current tab.

Option A — take the current tab rejected as default

Navigate the top frame to the bank. Come back to a stored return_url, merchant page reboots, widget re-mounts and rehydrates from the server.

Cost. You destroy the merchant's checkout page. Cart state, half-filled address form, promo code, all their JS state. On an account-linking widget that is annoying. At checkout it is a conversion disaster, and it would be the number one integration complaint you receive.

You still have to build this. It is the mobile path and the popup-blocked fallback. You do not get to delete the rehydration logic.

Option B — a popup default on desktop

window.open creates a new top-level window. It does not overlay your widget and it does not replace the tab. The merchant tab keeps running in the background with the iframe still mounted.

The gotcha. window.open must be called synchronously inside the user's click handler. Any await before it and the browser has lost the user-activation flag and the popup blocker eats it. So fetch the authorize URL before the click, or open a blank popup first and set popup.location once the URL arrives.

How the popup gets its answer home SSE, not BroadcastChannel

The SSE stream is the mechanism. The iframe opened GET /v1/widget/session/events at boot and held it through the whole OAuth leg. The popup posts the callback, the server burns, exchanges, commits authorized, and pushes on that stream. The iframe learns from the server, never from the popup.

The popup could not win a race anyway. It can only broadcast after its POST returns, and the server emitted on the stream before it wrote that response. A browser-to-browser message here is strictly later than the thing it claims to optimize.

BroadcastChannel is kept as an opportunistic path only, attempted and never depended on. window.opener.postMessage is not even that, since opener is frequently null under the Cross-Origin-Opener-Policy headers you want for other reasons.

Popup cancel is a separate mechanism. If the user closes the popup at the bank, no request reaches us, so the stream has nothing to say. The iframe called window.open and holds the handle, so it polls popup.closed. A window reference is not storage, so nothing below touches it.

Why BroadcastChannel is nearly dead here storage partitioning

Browsers key storage by the pair of top-level site and frame origin, not origin alone. That covers cookies, localStorage, sessionStorage, IndexedDB, SharedWorker and BroadcastChannel. Chrome shipped it in 2023, Safari has had it under ITP for longer, Firefox does it under Total Cookie Protection.

Apply it here. Our iframe is provider.com inside merchant.com, so its key is that pair. The popup is a top-level context on provider.com, so it is first-party. Same origin, different partitions. Two channels with the same name that cannot hear each other.

The failure is silent. No exception, no error event. You post and nothing ever arrives. And it works perfectly in development, because you test the widget embedded on your own domain, which is the one case where the partitions collapse. Partition inheritance for popups has also shifted between engine versions, so the practical description is that it sometimes works — acceptable for an optimization, disqualifying for a correctness path.

The Storage Access API is not a rescue. It needs a user gesture plus prior first-party interaction and is aimed at cookies.

What changes on mobile

  • Popups are unreliable. Mobile Safari in particular. Detect and fall back to the full-tab redirect.
  • You need return_url. On return, look up the session by oauth_state, read the stored and pre-validated return_url, 302 there with the session id appended.
  • Both pages have amnesia. The merchant page reboots and the widget SPA restarts empty. Recovery has two independent paths. sessionStorage breadcrumb for same-tab, and oauth_state in the URL for the case where storage is empty or the bank opened a new tab.
  • App-to-app. Many banks deep-link into their native app. You leave the browser entirely and come back through a universal link. Same recovery logic, which is why building it around the server-side oauth_state lookup rather than client storage is the right call.
Say this: "Same framing constraint as any OAuth widget, but at checkout the page I'd be destroying is worth a lot more, so I default to the popup on desktop and keep the redirect-and-rehydrate path for mobile."

14. Design choices to defend

Why Postgres

Money system, tiny working set, hard correctness needs. You want real unique constraints and multi-row transactions, because both the outbox and the nonce burn depend on writing two things atomically.

Peak is tens of payments per second. One primary handles it. Reaching for a distributed KV here trades away the exact guarantee the design rests on, for scale you do not need.

Two details to defend

amount_minor as an integer. Float money is a real bug. A cent of drift on a reconciliation report is an audit finding.

The partial unique index. User tries Chase, insufficient funds, tries Wells Fargo. One session, two attempts, one order, one reference. A plain unique(session_id) would forbid the retry.

The pattern. Push the invariant down to the layer that can enforce it. If-statements are hope, constraints are guarantees.

Why REST at the merchant edge

REST rather than gRPC because we do not control the client. Merchants are on PHP 7, Ruby, Java 8. gRPC belongs behind our own API layer, where we control both ends. The two-tier answer is the good one. REST at the edge because I do not control the client, gRPC internally because I do. GraphQL loses here, because a small fixed set of auditable idempotent mutations gets nothing from a query planner and pays for it in rate limiting and audit clarity.

And action endpoints beat a generic PATCH. POST /payment_sessions/{id}/cancel is not strictly RESTful, but PATCH with {state} implies the client may set arbitrary states. You would need a transition whitelist immediately, which is the action endpoints again with a bigger blast radius. Stripe has /cancel and /confirm for this reason.

15. How the bank gets data to our backend

There are three separate channels from the bank side, they arrive at different times, and conflating them is a common mistake. Draw them as three arrows, not one.

#ChannelCarriesTimingTrust level
1 Browser redirect to our redirect_uri code and state Seconds. Synchronous with the user. Untrusted. It came through a browser we do not control. Every value must be validated server-side.
2 Our server-to-server call to the bank's token endpoint We send code + our client secret, we get back the access token, account token, last4 Sub-second, we initiate it Trusted. Mutual TLS, our secret, the bank's certificate. This is the first thing in the flow we can believe.
3 Rail settlement, via our bank partner Accepted, settled, or a return code such as R01 Hours to days. Long after the user is gone. Trusted, signed or mTLS, but slow and out of order.

Channel 1 is the one that bites people

Everything arriving on the redirect passed through a browser. An attacker can craft that URL. So the callback handler must, in order, burn the nonce atomically, look up the session from the nonce row rather than from anything in the URL, and only then exchange the code.

Never trust a session_id, an amount, or a merchant id that arrived in a redirect query string. The only thing you take from that URL is an opaque token you can look up in your own database.

Channel 3 is push or pull, and it depends

Push. The bank partner POSTs to a signed endpoint we expose, or drops a NACHA return file into an SFTP bucket that triggers a processing job. Low latency, but you must handle duplicates and out-of-order delivery.

Pull. We poll the partner's status API on a schedule. Simpler and easier to reason about, and honestly fine here because ACH settles in days. Latency of a five-minute poll is noise next to a two-day settlement.

Do both, and say why. Push for latency, poll as a safety net so a missed webhook does not strand a payment forever. The reconciliation job from section 9 is the same job. On a slow rail, polling is the source of truth and push is the optimization, which is the inverse of how it works for the merchant-facing webhooks.

16. Accessibility and i18n

Rarely the main event, but on a frontend round an embeddable modal invites these questions, and having a real answer separates you from candidates who only did the backend.

Accessibility

  • Focus trap while open. Tab and Shift-Tab cycle inside the widget and never reach the merchant page behind it.
  • Focus returns to the trigger on close. Store the element that opened it, restore on destroy(). Otherwise focus resets to the top of the document and a keyboard user is lost.
  • ESC exits and fires onExit, same as the close button.
  • ARIA live region for state changes. This is the one that is specific to this design. The popup closes and the widget advances without any user action, so a screen reader user gets no announcement at all unless you push one. Same again when settlement flips the UI. aria-live="polite" on the status region.
  • role="dialog" with aria-modal="true" and a labelled heading, so assistive tech announces what opened.
  • Errors announced, not just coloured. aria-describedby on the failing control.
The iframe complicates the focus trap. The trapping code lives in the host SDK, but the focusable elements live inside a cross-document iframe the host cannot enumerate. So the widget manages focus internally and the SDK traps only at the iframe boundary. Worth naming, because it is a real consequence of the isolation choice.

i18n

  • Currency formatting is not string concatenation. Intl.NumberFormat(locale, {style:'currency', currency}) over amount_minor / 100. Symbol position, decimal separator, and grouping all differ by locale, and $1,234.56 is 1.234,56 € elsewhere.
  • Locale comes from the session, set by the merchant at creation, not sniffed from the browser. The merchant knows their customer better than navigator.language does.
  • Institution names are not translated. Chase is Chase. Translate chrome and instructions, never proper nouns.
  • RTL needs logical CSS properties and dir="rtl" on the widget document, and the iframe means you set it independently of the host page.
  • Error copy comes from the server as a machine-readable failure_code plus a localised string, so the widget never builds sentences from fragments.

If asked to prioritise. Focus trap and focus restoration first, because a keyboard user simply cannot complete the flow without them. The live region second, because of the silent async advance. Everything else is polish.

17. Follow-ups — answers to have ready

The provider backend goes down mid-flow. What does the user see?

Depends entirely on where. Before authorized, the widget shows a retry and nothing is lost, because no money moved. After payment_submitted, the debit is already on the rail and our database is the only record of it, so the answer is that this database must be replicated with synchronous commit before we ever call the rail. Fail closed. If we cannot durably record the intent, we must not submit. An unrecorded debit is unrecoverable money loss, whereas a rejected payment is a bad afternoon.

The bank's OAuth server is down or slow.

Fail open toward the user with a clear message, because nothing has been committed. Circuit-break per institution so one broken bank does not consume your connection pool, and mark it degraded in the institution list so users pick a working one instead of failing repeatedly. Session sits in bank_auth_pending until it expires at 30 minutes. This is the one place where per-dependency isolation really matters, since you integrate with thousands of banks with wildly different reliability.

The user closes the popup halfway through.

Nothing happens, which is the point of the design. The session stays in bank_auth_pending, the nonce expires in 10 minutes, the session expires in 30. The iframe can detect the closed popup by polling popup.closed and offer a retry, which mints a fresh nonce and invalidates the old one. No cleanup needed because nothing was committed.

Two tabs, same order, both open the widget.

The unique (merchant_id, merchant_reference) constraint means the merchant's second create either returns the same session through the idempotency key or fails. If they somehow get two sessions, the partial unique index still limits each session to one live debit, but the merchant could be charged twice for one order. That is a real gap and the merchant_reference uniqueness is the only thing that closes it, which means it depends on the merchant passing a stable reference. Volunteer this rather than waiting to be asked.

A webhook is delivered but the merchant returns 500 forever.

We retry for 24 hours, then stop and alert. Their order hangs, which is why GET /v1/events?after={cursor} exists as a pull-based recovery path. The deeper point is that a push-only integration is never complete. Any system whose correctness depends on the other party's uptime needs a pull path.

Hot spots or scale problems?

Not really on the payment path, since sessions are naturally sharded by merchant and there is no shared contended row. Two real ones. First, a flash sale at one large merchant concentrates load on one merchant's rows and on their webhook endpoint, so rate-limit outbound delivery per merchant and give large merchants their own delivery workers. Second, the institution search is read-heavy and identical for everyone, so cache it at the CDN. Neither is the interesting part of this system, which is worth saying so you do not burn time there.

You said succeeded is terminal, but ACH returns exist. Are you not lying to the merchant?

We are making them a promise and taking the risk, which is different from lying. Internally the Payment row still records a return and it flows to a loss ledger. What makes this viable is scoring before we submit, using balance from the bank connection we already have, account age, cross-merchant velocity, and device signals. If our loss rate exceeds what we priced into the fee, the business fails, not the architecture. That framing is the honest one.

Why not just use the current tab everywhere and skip the popup?

You could, and you must build that path anyway for mobile. I default to the popup on desktop purely because the page being destroyed is a live checkout with cart and form state, and that is a conversion cost the merchant will not accept. It is a UX trade, not a security one. Both are equally secure, since the bank is top-level either way.

What is the weakest part of your design?

The gap between committing submitting and the rail acknowledging. No database transaction spans it, so it is closed by a reconciliation sweeper rather than a guarantee, and the sweeper's correctness depends on the rail exposing lookup by our deterministic reference. If a partner did not support that, I would need to hold submission behind a durable queue with a bounded, manually reviewed retry, and accept latency to get certainty.

18. Numbers to drop

Load

  • Mid-size platform, 10M payments/month ≈ 4 payments/sec average, ~40/sec at Black Friday peak.
  • That is tiny. One Postgres primary handles it with room to spare, which is exactly why you should not shard.
  • Each payment is roughly 8 to 12 API calls across widget, callback and polling, so call it ~500 req/sec peak.
  • Institution search is the read hot spot at maybe 10x the payment rate, and it is fully cacheable.

Storage and time

  • PaymentSession ≈ 500 bytes. 10M/month ≈ 5 GB/month, ~60 GB/year. Trivial.
  • Nonce and idempotency rows are reaped, so they stay in the low millions.
  • Latency budget for the user. Widget boot under 500ms, institution search under 100ms, callback exchange 200 to 800ms driven by the bank, balance check 1 to 3s.
  • Latency of the money. Standard ACH 1 to 3 business days, same-day ACH by cutoff. Six orders of magnitude slower than the UI. That gap is the whole design.
  • Webhook fan-out at peak is ~120/sec across all merchants, which one worker pool handles.
